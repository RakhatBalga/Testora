# 3. Literature Review

## 3.1 Scope and search method

This chapter reviews the research that Testora builds on. Testora is an IELTS preparation system that grades a learner's answer, extracts mistakes, names the main reason the band score is stuck, assigns a task for the day, regrades the new attempt and shows how the band changed. Every step of this loop depends on an assessment or learner-modelling method that already has a research literature. The review therefore covers four themes:

1. automated essay scoring (AES), from feature-based systems to neural and LLM-based models;
2. large language models (LLMs) used as graders: agreement with human raters, prompt and rubric design, consistency and bias;
3. automated speaking assessment and automatic speech recognition (ASR);
4. adaptive learning: item response theory (IRT) and the Rasch model, knowledge tracing, and practice recommendation.

Sources were searched in the ACL Anthology (ACL, EMNLP and the BEA workshop series on NLP for educational applications), the ISCA Archive (SLaTE/Interspeech), PMLR and NeurIPS proceedings, the Educational Data Mining proceedings, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, ACM Digital Library and Google Scholar. The main keywords were: *automated essay scoring*, *ASAP*, *TOEFL11*, *IELTS writing LLM*, *GPT-4 essay scoring reliability*, *LLM-as-a-judge bias*, *automated speech scoring*, *SpeechRater*, *Whisper spoken language assessment*, *ASR word error rate disparities*, *Rasch model*, *item difficulty estimation*, *Bayesian knowledge tracing* and *deep knowledge tracing*. Every source was checked against its publisher page, DOI record or proceedings entry. Findings below are reported only to the extent they could be confirmed from the abstract or full text.

Because LLM-based assessment changes quickly, most sources are from 2021–2026. A small number of foundational works are kept because Testora still uses their models directly: the Rasch model (Rasch, 1960/1980), Bayesian knowledge tracing (Corbett & Anderson, 1994), the first neural AES model (Taghipour & Ng, 2016) and deep knowledge tracing (Piech et al., 2015). The official IELTS band descriptors (IELTS, 2023a; 2023b) are included as a non-scientific standard because they define the target scale.

## 3.2 Theme A: Automated essay scoring

### What is known

Automated essay scoring has a long history. Operational systems such as ETS's e-rater were built on hand-crafted linguistic features combined in a statistical scoring model. Attali and Burstein (2006) describe e-rater V.2, which uses a small, interpretable feature set, one scoring model that can be shared across prompts, and weights that can be set with expert judgement. The paper also reports validity and reliability evidence. This generation of systems established two ideas that still matter: scores must be checked against human raters, and features should map to aspects of writing that teachers recognise.

Taghipour and Ng (2016) were among the first to drop feature engineering altogether. Their recurrent neural network (best variant: LSTM) learned the essay-to-score relation directly on the Automated Student Assessment Prize (ASAP) dataset. It beat a strong feature-based baseline by 5.6% in quadratic weighted kappa (QWK). Their work made ASAP and QWK the standard benchmark and metric for AES research. Ramesh and Sanampudi (2022) later reviewed the field systematically and reached a critical conclusion: most systems model surface style, while relevance of the content to the prompt, development of ideas, cohesion and coherence are still poorly captured.

The move to LLMs has made it possible to score essays with a rubric and no task-specific training. Mizumoto and Eguchi (2023) used GPT-3 (text-davinci-003) to score all 12,100 essays in the ETS Corpus of Non-Native Written English (TOEFL11). The scores showed "a certain level of accuracy and reliability" against benchmark proficiency levels, and adding classic linguistic features improved accuracy further. For IELTS specifically, Qiu, Yan and Cai (2024) compared a fine-tuned RoBERTa model with prompted GPT-4o and Llama-3-70B-Instruct on publicly available IELTS essay datasets (a Kaggle set of about 1,200 essays and a HuggingFace set of about 10,000 Task 2 essays). Both the fine-tuned RoBERTa and GPT-4o reached a human–machine correlation above 0.7.

### Gaps and limitations

- **Holistic scores dominate.** Most AES work predicts a single overall score, while IELTS reports four analytic criteria. Ramesh and Sanampudi (2022) also show that content and coherence are the weakest-modelled aspects, and these are exactly Task Response and Coherence & Cohesion in IELTS.
- **Benchmark data are not IELTS data.** ASAP essays come from US school students, and TOEFL11 uses coarse proficiency levels. Public IELTS essay sets are small and scraped from the web, and the authors of Qiu et al. (2024) note that their score distributions are imbalanced. A model tuned on one of these sets may not transfer to a different learner population (domain shift).
- **Correlation is not enough.** A correlation of 0.7 can still hide systematic over- or under-scoring by half a band, which matters a great deal to an IELTS candidate. Mizumoto and Eguchi (2023) report reliability but, as Qiu et al. (2024) point out, did not show that the LLM is better than existing AES methods.

### Implication for Testora

Testora uses an LLM (Gemini) to score each IELTS criterion separately, rather than a single holistic score, so that the feedback loop can name the weakest criterion. Following Taghipour and Ng (2016) and later AES practice, the planned accuracy experiment uses QWK and mean absolute error (MAE), plus the share of essays within ±0.5 band of an expert score, which is the tolerance that matters on the IELTS scale. Following Attali and Burstein (2006) and Mizumoto and Eguchi (2023), the experiment will also include a baseline regression model on interpretable text features (length, lexical diversity, error counts, cohesive devices). This tests whether the LLM adds value over a cheap model, and whether combining the two improves accuracy. Because the public IELTS datasets are small and noisy, results on them are treated as indicative only; the main evaluation will use essays scored by human experts.

## 3.3 Theme B: LLMs as graders — reliability, rubric design and bias

### What is known

Several studies now measure how closely LLM grades agree with human raters in second-language (L2) assessment. Yancey et al. (2023) rated short L2 English essays from a high-stakes test on the CEFR scale with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. GPT-4 given calibration examples came close to established automated writing evaluation methods. However, agreement with human ratings varied with the test-taker's first language. Pack, Barrett and Escalante (2024) evaluated PaLM 2, Claude 2, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on 119 placement-test essays. Each model scored every essay twice, on two separate occasions, and a pair of human raters scored them too. GPT-4 performed best, with excellent intra-rater reliability and good validity. But all models showed changes over time: intra-rater reliability went up for most models, while the inter-rater reliability of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 decreased slightly. The authors conclude that "all models are subject to fluctuations in their performance."

Analytic (per-criterion) scoring has also been tested. Bannò et al. (2024) asked GPT-4, zero-shot, to produce analytic scores for L2 essays from a public CEFR-annotated dataset. The predicted analytic scores correlated significantly with features linked to the individual proficiency components. This suggests the model is not simply repeating one holistic judgement under four names. The same authors note that analytic scoring inherits flaws from human scoring, because human analytic ratings are themselves less reliable than holistic ones.

Prompt design also matters. Stahl et al. (2024) compared zero-shot and few-shot prompting strategies, with and without chain-of-thought, for joint essay scoring and feedback generation. Doing scoring and feedback together improved scoring performance. The scores had only a small effect on feedback quality, although human evaluators rated the feedback highly.

Research on LLM evaluators outside education reports systematic biases. Wang et al. (2024) showed that GPT-4 used as a judge is sensitive to the order in which candidate answers are presented. With ChatGPT as the evaluator, Vicuna-13B could appear better than ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries simply by changing positions. The authors propose calibration strategies (multiple evidence, balanced positions, human-in-the-loop) that reduce this bias.

### Gaps and limitations

- **Score variance and drift.** The same model can give different scores to the same text across runs and across model versions (Pack et al., 2024). Hosted models are updated without notice, so the scoring function can drift over time.
- **Bias.** Agreement depends on the writer's first language (Yancey et al., 2023), and LLM judges have position and other presentation biases (Wang et al., 2024). For Testora's users, who are mostly Kazakh and Russian speakers, no published study measures LLM-scoring bias for these first languages.
- **Most studies use GPT models and CEFR or placement-test scales.** We found no peer-reviewed study that evaluates Gemini on the four IELTS writing criteria against certified examiner scores.
- **Analytic labels are scarce.** Few public datasets have reliable per-criterion IELTS scores, so analytic accuracy is hard to measure (Bannò et al., 2024).

### Implication for Testora

Testora's grading prompt includes the public IELTS band descriptors for each criterion (IELTS, 2023a; 2023b) and asks the model for criterion scores together with the evidence (the mistakes) behind them, an approach close to the joint scoring-and-feedback setup of Stahl et al. (2024). Because of the variance shown by Pack et al. (2024), the planned experiment will grade each essay several times and report the spread of scores, not only accuracy, and the model version will be fixed and logged with every grade. To control rubric drift, a small set of anchor essays with expert scores will be re-graded whenever the model or prompt changes. Following Yancey et al. (2023), agreement will be broken down by learner subgroup where data allow. Until the experiment is done, Testora will present its band as an estimate for practice, not as a predicted official score.

## 3.4 Theme C: Automated speaking assessment and ASR

### What is known

The classic architecture for scoring spontaneous speech is a pipeline: an ASR system transcribes the response, features are computed from the recognition output and audio, and a statistical model maps the features to a score. Zechner et al. (2009) describe this approach for SpeechRater, the ETS system for scoring non-native spontaneous speech, which was used operationally in TOEFL Practice Online. Its design shows that delivery features (fluency, pauses, pronunciation) can be modelled separately from language-use features, and that automated speech scores must be validated against the construct of the test and not only correlated with human scores.

Modern ASR has improved greatly. Radford et al. (2023) trained Whisper on 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask, weakly supervised audio. The resulting models generalise well to standard benchmarks and are often competitive with fully supervised systems without any dataset-specific fine-tuning. Whisper is also released openly, which makes it a practical choice for a student project.

However, a general-purpose ASR model is not built for assessment. Ma et al. (2023) showed that Whisper is trained to produce *readable* transcripts: it adds punctuation, normalises numbers and abbreviations, and often leaves out disfluencies and hesitations. These are exactly the signals that fluency assessment needs. They showed that fine-tuning and soft prompt tuning can change Whisper's decoding so it outputs the exact words spoken. ASR accuracy is also uneven across speaker groups: Koenecke et al. (2020) found that five commercial ASR systems had an average word error rate (WER) of 0.35 for Black speakers against 0.19 for white speakers, and traced the gap to the acoustic models.

### Gaps and limitations

- **ASR errors spread into scores.** If ASR misses a filler or "corrects" a grammar error, the fluency and grammar scores computed from the transcript are wrong.
- **Accent bias.** Koenecke et al. (2020) studied US English dialects, not L2 speakers, but the mechanism (training data that under-represents some speaker groups) applies to Central Asian learners of English as well. We found no published WER figures for Whisper on Kazakh- or Russian-accented L2 English.
- **Transcript-only grading loses information.** An LLM that reads a clean transcript cannot hear pronunciation, intonation or pauses. IELTS Speaking includes a Pronunciation criterion and a Fluency and Coherence criterion (IELTS, 2023b), which need acoustic evidence.

### Implication for Testora

Testora's speaking component compares Whisper model sizes by WER and speed on recordings of its own users, and checks verbatim decoding settings so that fillers, repetitions and self-corrections are kept (Ma et al., 2023). Following the SpeechRater design (Zechner et al., 2009), Testora computes interpretable fluency features (speech rate, pause count and length, filler rate, self-corrections and ASR confidence) and trains a regression or gradient-boosting model to predict the speaking band. This model will be compared with LLM grading of the transcript and with expert scores. Low ASR confidence will be shown to the user and will lower the confidence of the grade, instead of silently producing a score from a faulty transcript. Pronunciation feedback will be described as limited until it is validated.

## 3.5 Theme D: Adaptive practice — IRT, knowledge tracing and recommendation

### What is known

Item response theory gives a probabilistic model of how a learner answers a test item. In the Rasch model (Rasch, 1960/1980), the probability of a correct answer depends only on the difference between the learner's ability and the item's difficulty, both on the same logit scale. This makes item difficulties comparable across learners and tests. Settles, LaFlair and Hagiwara (2020) showed how machine learning can support this in language testing: for the Duolingo English Test, they used linguistic models to estimate item difficulty for computer-adaptive testing, which reduced the need for costly pilot testing with human test-takers. The resulting scores were highly reliable and aligned with other high-stakes English tests.

Knowledge tracing models how a learner's mastery changes during practice. Bayesian knowledge tracing (BKT), introduced by Corbett and Anderson (1994) in cognitive tutors, treats each skill as a hidden binary state (mastered or not) and updates the probability of mastery after each answer using learning, guess and slip parameters. Piech et al. (2015) proposed deep knowledge tracing (DKT), which uses a recurrent neural network and does not need skills to be defined by hand. It gave substantial improvements in prediction on several datasets. Wilson et al. (2016) then showed that Bayesian extensions of IRT, especially hierarchical and temporal variants, generally matched or beat DKT on two public datasets and one proprietary dataset, while being simpler and easier to interpret. The survey by Abdelrahman, Wang and Nunes (2023) covers the field from early probabilistic models to recent deep models, and links knowledge tracing to applications such as learning-material recommendation.

### Gaps and limitations

- **Cold start.** IRT difficulties and BKT parameters need many responses per item and per learner. A new platform has few users, and every new learner starts with no history.
- **Complex models need big data.** DKT's advantages appear on large datasets, while Wilson et al. (2016) show simpler Bayesian models are competitive and more interpretable, which matters when the system must explain *why* it recommends a task.
- **Skill definitions.** BKT needs a skill map. For IELTS Reading and Listening, a natural unit is the question type (for example matching headings or True/False/Not Given), but these types are not pure skills.
- **Writing and speaking mistakes are not right/wrong items.** Knowledge tracing assumes binary answers; mistakes extracted from essays need a different, weighted representation.

### Implication for Testora

For Reading and Listening, Testora calibrates item difficulty with a regularised Rasch model. Regularisation (a prior that pulls estimates towards the mean) is used because of the small number of responses per item at launch. Mastery is tracked per question type with a Bayesian model in the spirit of BKT (Corbett & Anderson, 1994), which we chose over DKT given the data size and the need for explainable output (Wilson et al., 2016). New items can start from text-based difficulty estimates, following Settles et al. (2020), until enough responses are collected. Following standard practice in the knowledge-tracing literature (Abdelrahman et al., 2023), predictive quality will be evaluated by AUC on held-out answers and compared with a simple per-type error-rate baseline. For Writing and Speaking, the weakness model aggregates extracted mistakes with exponential recency weighting (half-life of 14 days), so recent mistakes count more than old ones. This rule is a design choice, not a result from the literature, and its parameters will be checked against user data.

## 3.7 Limitations Testora takes into account

The literature points to a set of known risks. Testora's design treats each of them explicitly:

| Limitation from the literature | Source(s) | How Testora handles it |
|---|---|---|
| LLM score variance across repeated runs | Pack et al. (2024) | Grade the same essay several times in the experiment; report the spread; keep generation settings fixed |
| Rubric / model drift as hosted models are updated | Pack et al. (2024) | Log model version with each grade; re-grade a fixed anchor set after any model or prompt change |
| Bias by first language and by prompt presentation | Yancey et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2024) | Subgroup analysis where data allow; fixed prompt structure; rubric grading of one answer at a time |
| Weak modelling of content and coherence | Ramesh & Sanampudi (2022) | Separate criterion scores with cited evidence (mistakes) for Task Response and Coherence & Cohesion |
| Domain shift (ASAP/TOEFL/CEFR data vs IELTS learners) | Taghipour & Ng (2016); Mizumoto & Eguchi (2023); Qiu et al. (2024) | Evaluate on IELTS essays scored by experts, not only on public datasets |
| Small labelled data for analytic IELTS scores | Bannò et al. (2024); Qiu et al. (2024) | Small expert-scored set; simple, regularised baseline models; results reported with confidence intervals |
| ASR errors and loss of disfluencies | Ma et al. (2023); Radford et al. (2023) | Compare Whisper sizes by WER; verbatim decoding; ASR confidence lowers grade confidence |
| ASR accuracy gaps for accented speech | Koenecke et al. (2020) | Measure WER on the platform's own (Kazakh/Russian L1) speakers before relying on transcripts |
| Cold start for item difficulty and learner mastery | Rasch (1960/1980); Settles et al. (2020); Corbett & Anderson (1994) | Regularised Rasch estimates; text-based difficulty priors; population-level mastery priors |
| Complex learner models are data-hungry and opaque | Piech et al. (2015); Wilson et al. (2016) | Bayesian per-type mastery instead of DKT; evaluate by AUC against an error-rate baseline |

The most important limitation is that, at the time of writing, Testora's own scoring accuracy has not been measured. The literature shows that results reported for other models, scales and learner groups cannot simply be transferred. For this reason the accuracy experiment (MAE, QWK, ±0.5-band agreement, run-to-run stability and comparison with a feature-based baseline) is part of the project plan, and claims about accuracy will be made only after it is complete.
